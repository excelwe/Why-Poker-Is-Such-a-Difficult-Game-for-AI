<p class="demoTitle">&nbsp;</p>
<p>When Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997 and AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in 2016, the programs were, at bottom, looking ahead. In a perfect-information game every player sees the whole board, so a program can take any position, search the moves that follow and evaluate the leaves.</p>
<p>Poker breaks the moment two cards go face down to each player. Noam Brown and Tuomas Sandholm at Carnegie Mellon put heads-up no-limit hold'em at 10^161 decision points, but the real problem is that the tree can't be cut up. Their Libratus paper puts it this way: "different parts of the game cannot be considered in isolation; the optimal strategy for a given situation may depend on the strategy that would be played in situations that have not occurred." Minimax has nothing to grab, because a node's value depends on what the opponent believes you hold, and that belief was shaped by branches never taken.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>No fixed board to solve</strong></strong></h2>
<p>A chess engine can treat the opponent as scenery, since whatever they play, the board after their move is fully known and the search restarts from it. In poker the opponent's habits are part of the position. The right play against someone who folds too much is wrong against someone who never folds, and those tendencies drift with stack depth and with everything they've watched you do. The field's best answer was to stop modeling the opponent at all. Counterfactual regret minimization, published by Zinkevich, Johanson, Bowling and Piccione in 2007, plays the game against itself over and over, tracks at each information set how much better each action would have done than its current strategy did, and shifts probability toward the actions with the most accumulated regret. In a two-player zero-sum game the average of those strategies converges toward a Nash equilibrium.</p>
<p>Cepheus, from the University of Alberta in 2015, is that idea at the limit, CFR+ run for roughly 900 core-years on heads-up limit hold'em until the remaining exploitability was below anything a human sample could detect. But limit poker has fixed bet sizes. No-limit adds a continuous action space, and the abstractions needed to shrink it are where a good human looks for holes.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>Bluffing and belief states</strong></strong></h2>
<p>A bluff is a bet whose meaning depends on what the opponent believes, and the DeepStack team described why that reasoning never bottoms out: "how our opponent's actions reveal that information depends upon their knowledge of our private information and how our actions reveal it." DeepStack's answer in 2017 was continual re-solving. Compute a strategy for the current situation only, carry forward your own range and the opponent's counterfactual values, and let networks trained on millions of generated situations stand in for the rest. Libratus, the same year, took 120,000 hands off four heads-up specialists at 147 milli-big-blinds per hand. Its subgame solving was safe in a specific sense: the opponent was held to the values they could already get against the precomputed blueprint, so re-solving never made the whole strategy more exploitable, and a third module ran overnight adding the bet sizes the humans had been using.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>Dynamic adaptation and counter-strategies</strong></strong></h2>
<p>In a two-player zero-sum game a Nash strategy is unexploitable, which isn't the same as profitable. Against an opponent making systematic mistakes the equilibrium leaves value on the table, and most steps toward exploiting them open a weakness of your own. It gets worse with more players. When <a href="https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aay2400"><u>Pluribus beat professionals</u></a>&nbsp;at six-player no-limit in 2019, its authors noted that in games with more than two players, even if a Nash equilibrium could be computed, "it is not clear that playing such an equilibrium strategy would be wise." Pluribus ran self-play with no equilibrium guarantee, won anyway, and its blueprint cost about $144 of cloud compute, eight days on a 64-core server.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>Solvers, bots and real-time assistance</strong></strong></h2>
<p>Compute that cheap doesn't stay in a lab. The same algorithms now run on laptops as solvers, used between sessions to study equilibrium play, and the line the industry draws is between studying and consulting during a hand. Real-time assistance, or RTA, is any tool that helps a decision while the hand is live, from a solver window to a preflop chart, and most sites treat it as cheating alongside bots, though where a permitted chart ends and a banned one begins varies by site.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>Machine learning as the security layer</strong></strong></h2>
<p>A live table throws off timing, bet sizes, session lengths and full hand histories, and the model that matters on the operator's side is the one reading that stream. It is a classifier with a brutal class imbalance. The events you care about are rare, and a false positive costs a real person their account, the same trade-off GitHub's security team describes for <a href="https://github.blog/security/vulnerability-research/leveraging-machine-learning-find-security-vulnerabilities/"><u>vulnerability detection</u></a>&nbsp;in code.</p>
<p><a href="https://wptglobal.com/"><u>WPT Global</u></a>&nbsp;reports that more than 99% of the integrity cases it acts on were surfaced by AceGuardian, a detection system built by A5 Labs, before any player complained. The denominator there is the caseload, and the question in anti-cheat is recall. A pipeline that generates the caseload will account for most of it, and it says nothing about the hands nobody flagged, which can't be counted because the model only sees behavior. A disciplined human on a heater and a bot on an ordinary night can produce the same timing and sizing for thousands of hands, and a player glancing at a solver on a second monitor produces nothing.</p>
<h2><strong><strong>What showdown gives you</strong></strong></h2>
<p>Fraud models have the same complaint. The adversary hides inside traffic that looks like everyone else's, and the labels arrive late, if at all, from chargebacks and complaints. Poker is the friendlier version. At showdown the cards turn over and you learn, for that one hand, whether the story matched the holding. Most hands never get there.</p>
